An essay on long-horizon manipulation

Bring the past
into the next action.

Beyond the current frame.

Moving from LIBERO and RoboTwin to RMBench changes more than how long a robot must act. It must remember earlier observations, track progress, and choose the next action even when its history becomes unreliable.

01

From “where is it?”
to “what has happened?”

Longer tasks amplify errors. Memory-dependent tasks change the information a decision requires.

In many familiar grasping, placement, and bimanual tasks, the current image and goal provide strong action cues: a cup on the table invites a grasp; an object outside its container still needs placing. RMBench specifically introduces manipulation that depends on past information: similar current observations may require very different next actions.[3]

Spatial, object, goal, and skill transfer

LIBERO

Tests manipulation and knowledge transfer across spatial relations, objects, and goals, including long-horizon task compositions.[1]

Illustrative example
Place a cup at a specified location. Its current state and the target location provide direct cues.
Bimanual coordination and variation

RoboTwin

Provides bimanual manipulation and scalable data generation. Version 2.0 emphasizes robustness under domain randomization.[2]

Illustrative example
Two arms cooperate to pick up, hand over, or place an object. Coordination, contact, and viewpoint changes can be central challenges.
History dependence and task progress

RMBench

Built on RoboTwin 2.0 to test memory dependence, including retaining initial information and tracking ongoing history.[3]

Press Button
Read two numbers, press the left and middle buttons the requested number of times, then confirm. The robot must track how many presses it has completed.
This comparison concerns benchmark emphasis. It does not divide benchmarks into short versus long tasks. LIBERO includes LIBERO-Long, and RoboTwin also has multistage tasks. RMBench explicitly tests memory dependence, beyond extending execution time.

Four complementary changes

Four ways to support longer tasks.

i.Look further aheadExtend the action chunk to cover a longer local behaviorii.Look back visuallyUse keyframe anchors to retain historical contextiii.Express progressPredict the subtasks for the upcoming action segmentiv.Expect imperfect historyReduce reliance on complete Previous text

These are design directions. They are not four independently proven necessities, nor does adding them one by one guarantee success.

02

Extend the chunk.
Give local behavior more room.

First ask how far a prediction reaches, then when to observe again.

A behavior such as approach, contact, press, and release may exceed a short action window. Extending it from 16 to 32 steps lets the model coordinate a longer segment in one prediction and moves ImageWAM’s endpoint-image supervision further into the future.[4]

However, an action chunk is a future-action window, not a history window. It does not tell the model how many presses have already occurred, and longer is not automatically better. Executing all 32 predicted steps before observing again also delays feedback and can allow more error to accumulate.

Prediction horizon H

The number of future actions generated at once. The example uses H=32 and training data with num_frames=33, including both endpoints from t to t+32; the action sequence still has 32 steps.

Execution horizon R

The number of actions executed before replanning. H and R can differ; this recipe uses 32/32. The interaction compares 16/16 with 32/32, without assuming alignment with semantic task boundaries.

03

Observe the present.
Anchor the past with keyframes.

Retain informative historical images as visual anchors, bringing earlier layouts and state changes into the current decision.

Keyframes can preserve information that later disappears.In Cover Blocks, blocks are covered in left-to-right order and then uncovered in a color-defined order. Once the covers hide the colors, keyframes can preserve the earlier color-to-position mapping. Keyframes can also record meaningful state changes.Comparing historical anchors with the current observation provides context for object states, arm motion, and contact stages. The idea is to record history through keyframes, without prescribing fixed sampling times or intervals.

Read historical anchors alongside the current observation: keyframes preserve layout and state; current feedback grounds the action.

These conceptual images illustrate layout and state anchors without fixing their number or sampling interval. Keyframes supply historical visual context; current observations supply execution feedback.

Keyframe anchors are not a complete event log.A brief press may occur between two frames. Seeing the states before and after it may not reveal how many successful presses occurred. Anchors must therefore be checked for the event information the task needs; a few historical images do not by themselves solve counting.
04

Predict the subtask.
Make the next segment explicit.

Turn a static goal into language conditioning that evolves with task progress.

“Complete the button task” is unchanged at every step. “Press the left button for the second time” expresses specific progress. The language branch uses images and Previous to predict Current, then passes its representation to the action branch to ground local behavior in a clearer stage.

In the window32 recipe, Current is not necessarily a single sentence. It describes the ordered subtasks within the future window and may cross a stage boundary. The model must represent this sequence, beyond recalling the overall goal.

Language can say “what I intend to do.”
Visual feedback must still answer: “did I actually do it?”

An easy boundary to overlook: during evaluation, Previous accumulates the model’s earlier generated plans, rather than an environment-verified completion log. If a press fails but the model records a second left press, the next plan may continue from an incorrect estimate of progress.

05

During training,
do not make history perfect.

Incomplete Teacher Forcing: practice making decisions with less history, instead of always continuing a complete demonstration.

Ground-truth Previous text is clean, coherent, and often strongly predictive of the next stage. A model may learn to continue that text without sufficiently using vision. During real evaluation, omissions, repetition, execution errors, and text truncation can all shift history away from demonstrations.

We therefore need to address the history distribution shift between training and evaluation. Incomplete-TF is one approach: with probability 0.3, truncate Previous to a valid but incomplete prefix while keeping Current supervision and image/action targets unchanged. The aim is to reduce reliance on perfect textual continuity.[4]

When perturbation is selected and history contains n sentences, sample k uniformly from {0,…,n−1} and retain the earliest k sentences. This is neither keeping the most recent sentences nor independently dropping each sentence. Evaluation still accumulates predicted history normally.

What it addresses

Missing history and incomplete progress cues, reducing dependence on a full Previous sequence. Current, action, and image supervision remain unchanged during training.

What it does not address

Incorrect content, reordered history, false completion claims, or all execution errors. It is not scheduled sampling with online generated history, and it does not verify that a button was actually triggered.

A motivation is not proof of necessity.Establishing the independent benefit of incomplete-TF requires ablations with matched budgets, initialization, and evaluation protocols. Its motivation alone does not establish a success-rate improvement.
06

Four adjustments.
Four information gaps.

Future reach, historical vision, stage representation, and history robustness should be considered together and evaluated separately.

One corresponding ImageWAM recipe combines chunk32 + historical keyframe anchors and current observations + window32 language prediction + incomplete Previous. This does not require stacking every subsequent variant: query changes how language is read; hist adds a text-history input; persistent visual memory is another mechanism for retaining observations.

Start with a clear baseline and vary one factor at a time. Use actual rollouts to check whether predicted progress follows execution. Low language CE, fluent sentences, or an oracle-assisted success alone do not establish that the long-horizon problem is solved.

What to verify next

A better next action starts
with a better reading of the past.

Time scales

Report prediction H and execution R. Chunk length is not memory length, and control steps are not seconds.

Information sources

Distinguish ground truth, predicted plans, and real observations. Future targets provide training supervision; they are not deployment inputs.

Error recovery

Check whether the model can recover correct progress after a missed or repeated step, beyond fitting clean demonstrations.

Comparable evaluations

Match tasks, budgets, initialization, seeds, and evaluation protocols. Report oracle-assisted, parallel, and partial evaluations separately.

Implementation conventions

Previous removes consecutive duplicate stage descriptions; Current window32 does so within the closed window [t,t+32]. Historical keyframes and current observations jointly provide visual context. Incomplete-TF runs only during training, with p=0.3, retaining a strict prefix. CE row weights are normalized by their sum; the global λ is applied separately. The interactions explain mechanisms and do not estimate success rates.
